In 2022, we came to Canada from Ukraine with two kids.

And almost all the money we had went into an old car.

Not a dream car. Not the car you put in a thumbnail and pretend life is perfect. Just an old car that could take us to work, to school, to groceries, and hopefully not surprise us too often.

We were renting. We had left our apartment back home. And then I started looking at houses.

Eight hundred thousand dollars. Nine hundred thousand. Sometimes one million.

I remember looking at those numbers and thinking, this is not difficult.

This is impossible.

And that feeling matters, because a lot of people talk about money like it is just math. Put this number here, invest this amount there, wait long enough, congratulations. But when you arrive in a new country, money is not only math.

Money is language. Rules. Credit history. Tax accounts. Rent. Insurance. A car you need before you even understand what the car really costs.

And on top of that, you are still building normal life.

Kids need stability. Groceries still need to be bought. The rent does not care that you are new here.

So this is not a video about how we became rich. We did not. This is not a video about which stock you should buy, or which ETF is perfect, or whether you should buy a house tomorrow.

This is a personal story about how we went from zero down payment to having options.

And honestly, options are underrated.

Because when you have no options, every price looks like a wall. When you start building capital, even slowly, the same world begins to look like a map.

Back in Ukraine, we had never really invested. About a year before moving, I had only a couple thousand dollars to experiment with. That was it. No deep experience. No magic strategy. No secret rich uncle explaining compound interest over dinner.

So when we arrived in Canada, I started researching like someone who was already late.

I wanted to understand the system. Not perfectly. Just enough to stop feeling completely blind.

What is a broker? What account should I open? What is the difference between saving and investing? Why does everyone talk about taxes? Why does credit score suddenly matter so much? What is employer matching? What is cashback? Why does every bank have three products that sound almost the same?

It was a lot.

And at the beginning, the more I learned, the more behind I felt.

That is one of the strange parts of immigration. You can be a capable adult, but the financial system can make you feel like a beginner again.

But eventually, I found a useful idea.

You do not need to understand everything to start. You need to understand enough to start safely.

For investing, one of those beginner-friendly tools was ETFs.

An ETF is basically a basket. Instead of trying to pick one perfect company, you can buy a basket that owns many companies inside it. That does not mean it is risk free. It is still investing. The value can go down. But the idea is simple enough for a beginner to understand.

And simple matters when your life is already full.

I personally became interested in investing. I liked reading about companies. I liked looking at businesses and asking, does this make sense? So for my own accounts, I bought individual stocks.

My wife did not want to do that.

And that is completely reasonable.

Not everyone wants to spend their evenings reading earnings reports. Some people have healthier hobbies. Apparently.

So for her accounts, I chose the simpler ETF-style path. Baskets instead of stock picking.

Same family goal. Different level of complexity.

That became one of the first lessons for us: the best system is not the most impressive system. The best system is the one the person can actually keep using.

After rent, insurance, the old car, groceries, kids, phone bills, and all the normal expenses, we did not have some huge amount left over.

But we decided to invest one hundred and fifty dollars a week.

That was less than I could make in a day.

And when you compare one hundred and fifty dollars to a house that costs eight hundred thousand, it feels almost ridiculous.

Like trying to fight a forest fire with a water bottle.

At the beginning, it felt pointless.

You put money in. The account still looks small. Then the market moves and sometimes it looks even smaller. You check again, as if staring at the number will make it behave better.

Nothing dramatic happens.

Nobody claps.

You still drive the same old car. You still rent. You still look at the house prices and think, who exactly is buying these?

But this is where the boring part becomes important.

A lot of financial progress does not feel like progress while it is happening.

It feels like repetition.

Every week, a small amount. Every month, learning one more thing. Every year, a little more confidence. Not because we became experts, but because the system became less invisible.

We learned about investment accounts. We learned about brokers. We learned that employer matching can matter. We learned that cashback is not a wealth strategy, but if you are already spending money, you might as well stop leaving small benefits on the table.

We learned that credit is not just a score; it affects real options.

We learned that taxes are not a topic you can ignore forever and then politely ask them to be simple.

And each of those things deserves its own video, because each one changed how I looked at money after immigration.

But the main engine was still boring.

One hundred and fifty dollars a week.

Not perfect. Not always exciting. Not always comfortable.

Just repeatable.

Four years later, my investment accounts are around fifty eight thousand six hundred dollars.

My wife's accounts are around fifty four thousand five hundred dollars.

Together, that is about one hundred and thirteen thousand dollars.

These numbers are not here to impress anyone. They are here because they changed the question.

Recently, we saw a similar house near the one we rent listed for around five hundred twenty seven thousand dollars.

And for the first time, the question was not, how could we ever buy a home?

The question became, should we buy one now, or keep growing?

That is a completely different kind of problem.

The first problem feels like a locked door.

The second problem feels like standing at a crossroads.

And I would rather have the crossroads.

Now, there are important details. Having more than twenty percent of a purchase price does not mean buying is automatically the right decision. There are closing costs. Mortgage payments. Maintenance. Interest rates. Taxes. Opportunity cost. The possibility that continuing to invest could be better. The possibility that stability for the family matters more.

This is why I do not want this channel to be about pretending money decisions are obvious.

They are not.

Especially after immigration.

A car decision is not just a car decision. It can affect work, insurance, cash flow, and stress.

A broker decision is not just an app. It can affect fees, habits, taxes, and what kind of investor you become.

A house is not just a house. It is a lifestyle decision, a financial decision, and sometimes an emotional decision wearing a roof.

So the lesson from our first four years is not, copy us.

Please do not copy strangers on the internet. Even friendly ones with subtitles.

The lesson is this.

If you are starting from zero in a new country, the first goal is not to look rich.

The first goal is to understand the system well enough to build options.

Start with one repeatable habit. Learn one tool at a time. Keep the plan simple enough that real life does not destroy it by Tuesday.

For us, that habit was investing one hundred and fifty dollars a week. For someone else, it may start with building an emergency fund, paying down expensive debt, improving credit, using employer match, or just tracking where the money actually goes.

The number matters less than the direction.

Because once you have capital, time starts working differently.

Small decisions stop being small. The broker matters. The account type matters. Fees matter. Taxes matter. Cashback matters. Employer match matters. Your ability to wait matters.

And maybe most importantly, your confidence changes.

Not loud confidence. Not the kind where you suddenly think you are a genius because the market went up.

Quiet confidence.

The kind where you can look at a difficult financial decision and say, okay, I may not know everything, but I know enough to ask better questions.

That is what this channel is about.

Money after immigration.

The mistakes. The tools. The accounts. The car decisions. The house decisions. The investing path. The things I wish someone explained earlier, in normal language, without acting like everyone already knows the rules.

In the next videos, I want to break down the exact tools that helped us: the broker setup, the ETF path, how we thought about individual stocks, employer matching, cashback, credit, and the question we are facing now.

Do we buy the first home, or keep growing wealth?

I do not know the final answer yet.

But four years ago, we did not even have the question.

And that is the change.

We went from zero to options.
